\chapter{Introduction}\label{chap:introduction}

Bayesian optimization (BO) is an efficient method for optimizing expensive-to-evaluate black-box functions. It has been widely applied in various fields, including hyperparameter tuning \citep{pmlr-v54-klein17a, WU201926}, materials discovery \citep{10.1039/c9sc04026a, 10.1039/d2nr07147a}, and robotics \citep{calandra2016bayesian, berkenkamp2023bayesian}. The BO framework consists of two core components: a surrogate model and an acquisition function \citep{frazier2018tutorial}. The surrogate model approximates the objective function based on the observations collected so far. It provides both a predictive mean that estimates the objective and a predictive variance that quantifies the uncertainty. The acquisition function then uses these predictions to determine the next point to evaluate. At each iteration, BO first fits the surrogate to the current data then maximizes the acquisition function to select the next query point. The objective function is evaluated at this point, and the resulting observation is added to the dataset. Since evaluating the acquisition function is far cheaper than evaluating the objective function, BO can make efficient use of a limited evaluation budget by concentrating on promising regions of the search space.

Gaussian processes (GPs) are among the most widely used surrogate models \citep{10.1145/3582078}. They provide both a predictive mean and a predictive variance, which acquisition functions use to select the next candidates by balancing exploration and exploitation. However, GP-based optimization suffers from several well-known limitations. Its performance depends heavily on the choice of kernel and hyperparameters, which often require manual specification or expensive optimization \citep{LI2026114819}. Moreover, they are struggle to capture non-stationary objective functions \citep{pmlr-v32-snoek14}. Their computational cost can also become prohibitive as the number of observations increases \citep{rasmussen2003gaussian}. Alternative surrogate models, such as random forests \citep{10.1007/978-3-642-25566-3_40}, Bayesian neural networks \citep{NIPS2016_a96d3afe}, and deep kernel learning \citep{pmlr-v37-snoek15}, address some of these limitations but introduce their own challenges. Specifically, random forests estimate uncertainty from the variance across individual trees, which is a heuristic that is often poorly calibrated and produces piecewise-constant predictions that extrapolate poorly away from observed data. Bayesian neural networks require costly approximate inference (e.g., stochastic gradient MCMC) and introduce many additional design choices, such as network architecture, priors, and sampler settings. Finally, deep kernel learning jointly trains a neural feature extractor with a GP kernel, which adds architecture-related hyperparameters and is prone to overfitting when only a handful of observations are available.
