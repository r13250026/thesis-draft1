\chapter{Introduction}\label{chap:introduction}

%Bayesian optimization (BO) is an efficient method for optimizing expensive-to-evaluate black-box functions. It has been widely applied in various fields, including hyperparameter tuning \citep{pmlr-v54-klein17a, WU201926}, materials discovery \citep{10.1039/c9sc04026a, 10.1039/d2nr07147a}, and robotics \citep{calandra2016bayesian, berkenkamp2023bayesian}. The BO framework consists of two core components: a surrogate model and an acquisition function \citep{frazier2018tutorial}. The surrogate model approximates the objective function based on the observations collected so far. It provides both a predictive mean that estimates the objective and a predictive variance that quantifies the uncertainty. The acquisition function then uses these predictions to determine the next point to evaluate. At each iteration, BO first fits the surrogate to the current data then maximizes the acquisition function to select the next query point. The objective function is evaluated at this point, and the resulting observation is added to the dataset. Since evaluating the acquisition function is far cheaper than evaluating the objective function, BO can make efficient use of a limited evaluation budget by concentrating on promising regions of the search space.

%Gaussian processes (GPs) are among the most widely used surrogate models \citep{10.1145/3582078}. They provide both a predictive mean and a predictive variance, which acquisition functions use to select the next candidates by balancing exploration and exploitation. However, GP-based optimization suffers from several well-known limitations. Its performance depends heavily on the choice of kernel and hyperparameters, which often require manual specification or expensive optimization \citep{LI2026114819}. Moreover, they are struggle to capture non-stationary objective functions \citep{pmlr-v32-snoek14}. Their computational cost can also become prohibitive as the number of observations increases \citep{rasmussen2003gaussian}. Alternative surrogate models, such as random forests \citep{10.1007/978-3-642-25566-3_40}, Bayesian neural networks \citep{NIPS2016_a96d3afe}, and deep kernel learning \citep{pmlr-v37-snoek15}, address some of these limitations but introduce their own challenges. Specifically, random forests estimate uncertainty from the variance across individual trees, which is a heuristic that is often poorly calibrated and produces piecewise-constant predictions that extrapolate poorly away from observed data. Bayesian neural networks require costly approximate inference (e.g., stochastic gradient MCMC) and introduce many additional design choices, such as network architecture, priors, and sampler settings. Finally, deep kernel learning jointly trains a neural feature extractor with a GP kernel, which adds architecture-related hyperparameters and is prone to overfitting when only a handful of observations are available.

However, these approaches introduce their own challenges, such as additional hyperparameters, computationally expensive training, or less reliable uncertainty estimates, motivating further research into more effective surrogate models for Bayesian optimization.


\section{Background and Motivation}

Many problems in science and engineering require optimizing a function that can only be evaluated by running an expensive procedure. Examples include selecting the configuration of a machine learning model, whose quality is known only after the model has been trained \citep{pmlr-v54-klein17a, WU201926}, and choosing the composition and processing conditions of a material, whose properties are known only after an experiment has been carried out \citep{10.1039/c9sc04026a, 10.1039/d2nr07147a}. Such functions are black boxes: they have no known analytic form, their derivatives are unavailable, and the evaluation budget is limited to tens or hundreds of queries. Bayesian optimization (BO) is a well-established strategy for this setting \citep{7352306, frazier2018tutorial}. It builds a probabilistic surrogate model of the objective from the evaluations collected so far and uses it to decide where to evaluate next.

In many of these problems, the search space combines continuous and categorical variables. The configuration of a machine learning model includes continuous variables such as the learning rate, together with categorical choices such as the type of optimizer or the activation function \citep{pmlr-v119-ru20a}. The design of a material may combine continuous quantities such as temperature or concentration with the choice of a constituent from a finite list \citep{zhang2020bayesian}. Such mixed search spaces are the setting of this thesis.

The Gaussian process (GP) is the standard surrogate in BO  \citep{rasmussen2006gaussian}. Its extension to categorical inputs requires a kernel that specifies how similar two configurations are, and a family of kernels and methods has been developed for this purpose \citep{pmlr-v119-ru20a, pmlr-v139-wan21b, pmlr-v206-deshwal23a}. Recently, tabular foundation models (TFMs) such as TabPFN \citep{hollmann2022tabpfn, hollmann2025accurate} have offered a different route. A TFM is pretrained on a large collection of synthetic datasets and then predicts on a new dataset in a single forward pass, without task-specific training, while accepting numerical and categorical features directly. TabPFN has already been used as a surrogate in BO for continuous problems \citep{ICLR2026_b54bafb9, Rogers2026}. Whether it is a good surrogate in mixed spaces has not been examined.

\section{Challenges of Mixed-Variable BO}

Mixed search spaces raise difficulties at every stage of the BO loop. Standard BO was developed mainly for continuous spaces, and its components do not carry over directly when some variables are categorical.

The central difficulty is that the levels of a nominal variable have no natural order or metric. Most surrogate models rely on the assumption that nearby inputs yield similar outputs, and this assumption has no meaning when level 3 is neither closer to level 4 than to level 9 nor related to them in any ordered way. A surrogate for a mixed space therefore has to specify how information is shared between levels, either explicitly through a similarity measure or implicitly through a learned structure.

This requirement is made harder by the size of the categorical part of the space, which contains $\prod_{j=1}^{m} K_j$ distinct configurations for $m$ categorical variables with $K_j$ levels. With three variables of five levels each there are 125 configurations, and with ten levels each there are 1000. Since the evaluation budget in BO is typically tens to a few hundred, many configurations are never evaluated, and exhaustive enumeration is not feasible. The surrogate must therefore generalize across configurations from very few observations, which makes the problem as much a statistical one as an optimization one.

The effect of a categorical variable on the objective may be a simple shift, or it may change the shape of the objective over the continuous variables. In the second case, the best continuous setting depends on the category selected, and a surrogate that models the two parts independently cannot represent the objective. The difficulty of a problem therefore depends not only on the number of categorical variables and their levels, but also on the strength of this interaction.

Finally, categorical variables complicate the use of the surrogate. A common way to feed them to a model that expects numeric input is one-hot encoding, which increases the input dimension and places the data on a set of isolated points. In addition, the acquisition function can no longer be maximized with gradient-based methods, since its gradient is not defined for categorical variables, and the maximization becomes a joint continuous and combinatorial problem.

\section{Research Questions and Contributions}

This thesis studies tabular foundation models as surrogates for BO in mixed search spaces. It replaces the GP surrogate with TabPFN. The thesis addresses two research questions.

\textbf{RQ1.} Can TabPFN serve as an effective surrogate for BO in mixed search spaces, in comparison with GP surrogates using kernels designed for categorical inputs and with non-BO baselines?

\textbf{RQ2.} How does the relative performance of TabPFN depend on the characteristics of the problem, namely the number of categorical variables, the number of levels per variable, the strength of the interaction between categorical and continuous variable?s%, and [the level of observation noise]?

The contributions of this thesis are as follows.

\begin{itemize}
    \item A systematic comparison of a tabular foundation model with Gaussian process surrogates and non-BO baselines on mixed-variable problems, on synthetic benchmark functions and real tasks.
    \item An analysis of how the comparison depends on the structure of the search space, which identifies the conditions under which each surrogate is preferable.
