\chapter{Preliminaries}\label{chap:preliminaries}

\section{Bayesian Optimization}\label{sec:bayesian-optimization}

Consider the problem of maximizing a black-box function $f: \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}$ over a mixed search space that combines continuous and categorical variables. A point is denoted as $x = (x_c, x_d)$, where $x_c$ contains $d_c$ continuous variables and $x_d$ contains $m$ categorical variables. The search space is

\begin{equation*}
  \mathcal{X} = \mathcal{X}_c \times \mathcal{X}_d, \qquad \mathcal{X}_c \subset \mathbb{R}^{d_c}, \qquad \mathcal{X}_d = \prod_{j=1}^{m} \{1, \dots, K_j\},
  \label{eq:search-space}
\end{equation*}

where $\mathcal{X}_c$ is compact and the $j$-th categorical variable takes one of $K_j$ levels. The levels are nominal, with no natural ordering or distance between them. The goal is to find

\begin{equation}
  x^* = \arg\max_{x \in \mathcal{X}} f(x).
  \label{eq:optimization-problem}
\end{equation}

Purely continuous and purely categorical problems are special cases of this formulation. Integer and ordinal variables can be treated as either continuous or categorical variables.

The function $f$ is a black box: it has no known analytic form and its gradient is unavailable. It can be accessed only by querying a chosen point $x$, which returns an observation corrupted by noise,

\begin{equation}
  y = f(x) + \epsilon, \qquad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2),
  \label{eq:observation-model}
\end{equation}

where the noiseless case corresponds to $\sigma^2 = 0$. Each evaluation is often expensive. Examples include training a machine learning model and characterizing material properties, so that the total budget is often limited to tens or hundreds of evaluations. Such problems are referred to as expensive black-box optimization. In a mixed space, this budget may be smaller than the number of categorical combinations $\prod_{j=1}^{m} K_j$.

Gradient-based methods, such as gradient descent and Newton's method, require derivative information and are therefore inapplicable. In a mixed space they are further restricted because the gradient is not even defined for categorical variables. Derivative-free methods rely only on function values and can be used instead. These include grid search and random search \citep{JMLR:v13:bergstra12a}, direct search methods such as Nelder-Mead \citep{10.1093/comjnl/7.4.308}, and population-based methods such as CMA-ES \citep{6790628}. However, none of these methods builds a global model of $f$. Grid search and random search ignore past evaluations when choosing the next point, and the other methods use only the current candidates or a summary of them to decide the next step. As a result, hundreds or thousands of evaluations are often required to converge, which is incompatible with the budget of expensive black-box problems. A method for this setting should instead make use of all past observations to decide where to evaluate next.

Bayesian optimization (BO) is a sequential, model-based strategy designed for this setting \citep{Močkus1975, jones1998efficient, 7352306, frazier2018tutorial}. Instead of probing $f$ blindly, BO maintains a probabilistic belief about $f$ and updates it after every evaluation. Let $\mathcal{D}_n = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ denote the observations collected after $n$ evaluations. At each iteration, BO uses $\mathcal{D}_n$ to decide where to query next in two steps:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Surrogate model.} A probabilistic model is fitted to $\mathcal{D}_n$ and yields a predictive distribution $p(y \mid x, \mathcal{D}_n)$ at any candidate point $x \in \mathcal{X}$. This distribution summarizes both the predicted value of $f(x)$ through its mean and the associated uncertainty through its spread. Because the surrogate is cheap to evaluate, it serves as an approximation to the expensive $f$.
  \item \textbf{Acquisition function.} A function $\alpha(x; \mathcal{D}_n)$ computed from the predictive distribution scores each candidate point by how useful it would be to evaluate next. Points with a promising predicted value receive high scores, which reflects exploitation, and so do points with high uncertainty, which reflects exploration. The next query point is
  \begin{equation}
    x_{n+1} = \arg\max_{x \in \mathcal{X}} \alpha(x; \mathcal{D}_n).
    \label{eq:next-query}
  \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

%Since $\alpha$ is inexpensive, the inner maximization costs far less than a single evaluation of $f$. In a mixed space this maximization is itself a mixed continuous and combinatorial problem, which is treated in \Cref{subsec:acquisition-optimization-mixed-spaces}. The overall procedure is summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:bo}.

Since $\alpha$ is inexpensive, the inner maximization costs far less than a single evaluation of $f$. The overall procedure is summarized in \Cref{alg:bo}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
  \caption{Bayesian Optimization}
  \label{alg:bo}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require evaluation budget $N$, initial design size $n_0$, surrogate model, acquisition function $\alpha$
    \State Sample initial points $\boldsymbol{x}_1, \dots, \boldsymbol{x}_{n_0} \in \mathcal{X}$, evaluate $y_i = f(\boldsymbol{x}_i) + \epsilon_i$, and set $\mathcal{D}_{n_0} = \{(\boldsymbol{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n_0}$
    \For{$n = n_0, \dots, N-1$}
      \State Fit the surrogate to $\mathcal{D}_n$ to obtain $p(y \mid \boldsymbol{x}, \mathcal{D}_n)$
      \State Select $\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1} = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathcal{X}} \alpha(\boldsymbol{x}; \mathcal{D}_n)$
      \State Evaluate $y_{n+1} = f(\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}) + \epsilon_{n+1}$
      \State Update $\mathcal{D}_{n+1} = \mathcal{D}_n \cup \{(\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}, y_{n+1})\}$
    \EndFor
    \Ensure the best observed point, $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^* = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{(\boldsymbol{x}_i, y_i) \in \mathcal{D}_N} y_i$
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The separation of the surrogate from the acquisition function is a key design feature. The surrogate encodes the belief about $f$, while the acquisition function encodes how to act on that belief. The two components are modular: any surrogate that returns a predictive distribution can be paired with any acquisition function. This thesis exploits this modularity by replacing the surrogate while keeping the rest of the BO loop unchanged. \Cref{fig:modular} shows the modular components of BO.

\section{Surrogate Models}\label{sec:surrogate-models}

The surrogate model turns the observations $\mathcal{D}_n$ into a belief about $f$. For use within BO, it has to return a predictive distribution $p(y \mid \boldsymbol{x}, \mathcal{D}_n)$ rather than a point estimate, since the uncertainty drives exploration. In a mixed space it must in addition handle categorical inputs. This section reviews two surrogates: the Gaussian process, which is the standard choice, and tabular foundation models, which have recently emerged as an alternative.

\subsection{Gaussian Process}\label{subsec:gaussian-process}

A Gaussian process (GP) is a distribution over functions such that any finite collection of function values is jointly Gaussian \citep{rasmussen2006gaussian}. It is written as

\begin{equation}
  f(\boldsymbol{x}) \sim \mathcal{GP}\bigl(\mu_0(\boldsymbol{x}),\, k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}')\bigr),
  \label{eq:gp-prior}
\end{equation}

where $\mu_0(\boldsymbol{x}) = \mathbb{E}[f(\boldsymbol{x})]$ is the prior mean function and $k(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{x}') = \mathrm{Cov}(f(\boldsymbol{x}), f(\boldsymbol{x}'))$ is the kernel. The mean is commonly set to a constant after standardizing the data, so the kernel carries most of the modelling assumptions. In a mixed space, the kernel has to define similarity for both parts of $\boldsymbol{x} = (\boldsymbol{x}_c, \boldsymbol{x}_d)$.

\textbf{Kernels for the continuous part.} Common choices are the squared exponential kernel and the Matérn family of kernels \citep{rasmussen2006gaussian}, usually with one length-scale per dimension. These kernels encode smoothness and a decay of correlation with distance, and are written $k_c(\boldsymbol{x}_c, \boldsymbol{x}_c')$ below.

\textbf{Kernels for the categorical part.} Distance has no meaning between nominal levels, so kernels for categorical inputs measure only whether levels match. The overlap kernel \citep{pmlr-v119-ru20a} is

\begin{equation}
  k_d(\boldsymbol{x}_d, \boldsymbol{x}_d') = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{j=1}^{m} \lambda_j \, \delta\big(x_{d,j},\, x_{d,j}'\big),
  \label{eq:kernel-discrete}
\end{equation}

where $\delta$ is the Kronecker delta and $\lambda_j \ge 0$ is a weight that plays the role of an automatic relevance determination parameter. It measures the extent to which two inputs share the same levels, and a variable with a small $\lambda_j$ contributes little to the similarity. The transformed overlap kernel \citep{pmlr-v139-wan21b} applies the exponential function to the overlap kernel, which increases its expressive power. \citet{pmlr-v206-deshwal23a} proposed the Hamming embedding via dictionary kernel (HED), which transforms categorical configurations into continuous representations to enable the use of standard kernel functions. % The diffusion kernel (Oh et al., 2019) defines similarity through a graph over categorical configurations and is designed for purely categorical problems.

\textbf{Combining the two parts.} The continuous and categorical kernels can be combined by a sum, a product, or a mixture of the two. A sum shares a common trend over the continuous variables across all categorical configurations, whereas a product allows the two parts to interact but vanishes when two points share no categorical level. A mixture,

\begin{equation}
  k(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{x}') = (1-\gamma)\big(k_c(\boldsymbol{x}_c, \boldsymbol{x}_c') + k_d(\boldsymbol{x}_d, \boldsymbol{x}_d')\big) + \gamma\, k_c(\boldsymbol{x}_c, \boldsymbol{x}_c')\, k_d(\boldsymbol{x}_d, \boldsymbol{x}_d'),
  \label{eq:kernel-mixed}
\end{equation}

where $\gamma \in [0,1]$ controls how strongly the categorical and continuous parts interact \citep{pmlr-v119-ru20a}.

\textbf{Posterior and hyperparameters.} Let $\boldsymbol{X} = (\boldsymbol{x}_1, \dots, \boldsymbol{x}_n)^\top$, $\boldsymbol{y} = (y_1, \dots, y_n)^\top$, $K = [k(\boldsymbol{x}_i, \boldsymbol{x}_j)]_{i,j}$, and $\boldsymbol{k}(\boldsymbol{x}) = (k(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{x}_1), \dots, k(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{x}_n))^\top$. The posterior of $f(\boldsymbol{x})$ given $\mathcal{D}_n$ is Gaussian with

\begin{align}
  \mu_n(\boldsymbol{x}) &= \mu_0(\boldsymbol{x}) + \boldsymbol{k}(\boldsymbol{x})^\top (K + \sigma^2 I)^{-1} (\boldsymbol{y} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0), \label{eq:gp-posterior-mean} \\
  \sigma_n^2(\boldsymbol{x}) &= k(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{x}) - \boldsymbol{k}(\boldsymbol{x})^\top (K + \sigma^2 I)^{-1} \boldsymbol{k}(\boldsymbol{x}), \label{eq:gp-posterior-variance}
\end{align}

where $\boldsymbol{\mu}_0 = (\mu_0(\boldsymbol{x}_1), \dots, \mu_0(\boldsymbol{x}_n))^\top$. The predictive distribution of a new noisy observation is $\mathcal{N}(\mu_n(\boldsymbol{x}), \sigma_n^2(\boldsymbol{x}) + \sigma^2)$. The hyperparameters $\boldsymbol{\theta} = (\sigma_f^2, \ell_1, \dots, \ell_{d_c}, \lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m, \sigma^2)$ are typically estimated by maximizing the log marginal likelihood,

\begin{equation}
  \log p(\boldsymbol{y} \mid \boldsymbol{X}, \boldsymbol{\theta}) = -\tfrac{1}{2} (\boldsymbol{y} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)^\top (K + \sigma^2 I)^{-1} (\boldsymbol{y} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0) - \tfrac{1}{2} \log \lvert K + \sigma^2 I \rvert - \tfrac{n}{2} \log 2\pi.
  \label{eq:gp-log-marginal-likelihood}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Limitations.} Despite its popularity, the GP faces several difficulties in a mixed space.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{Fixed notion of similarity.} The overlap kernel treats every pair of differing levels as equally dissimilar. Structure among levels, such as two levels that behave alike, can be learned only through the data, which are scarce.
  \item \textit{Many hyperparameters from few observations.} Each continuous dimension and each categorical variable has its own parameter. The marginal likelihood \eqref{eq:gp-log-marginal-likelihood} is non-convex in $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ and can be poorly identified from few observations, so the fitted surrogate can be sensitive to the estimation procedure.
  \item \textit{Assumed interaction structure.} The choice among product, sum, and mixture kernels encodes an assumption about how the categorical variables change the function over the continuous variables. A wrong assumption can mislead the search.
  \item \textit{Cost and dimensionality.} Evaluating the posterior \eqref{eq:gp-posterior-mean}–\eqref{eq:gp-posterior-variance} and the marginal likelihood \eqref{eq:gp-log-marginal-likelihood} costs $O(n^3)$ time. This is rarely an issue for tens of observations but grows with the budget, and kernel-based similarity degrades as the dimension increases.
\end{itemize}

These difficulties motivate surrogates that require less manual specification of similarity between inputs.

\subsection{Tabular Foundation Models}\label{subsec:tabular-foundation-models}

The difficulties of the GP share a common source. The similarity between inputs has to be specified by the modeller through a kernel, and the kernel parameters have to be estimated from few observations. Tabular foundation models (TFMs) take a different route. A single network is pretrained on a large collection of synthetic datasets and then makes predictions for a new dataset without task-specific training. The best-known family is TabPFN \citep{hollmann2022tabpfn, hollmann2025accurate}, whose releases have grown from small classification problems to large tables with many features. The most recent is TabPFN-3.5 \citep{jager2026tabpfn}, and this thesis uses its open-weight checkpoint.

\textbf{Prior-data fitted networks.} TabPFN builds on the prior-data fitted network (PFN) framework \citep{muller2021transformers}. Let $p(\mathcal{D})$ denote a prior over datasets, obtained by sampling data-generating processes from a synthetic prior. A transformer $q_\phi$ is trained to predict a held-out label given the remaining observations as context:

\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_{(\mathcal{D}_n,\, x,\, y) \sim p}\big[-\log q_\phi(y \mid x, \mathcal{D}_n)\big].
\end{equation}

The expected cross-entropy is minimized when $q_\phi(y \mid x, \mathcal{D}_n)$ equals the posterior predictive distribution $p(y \mid x, \mathcal{D}_n)$ under the prior. A trained PFN therefore approximates Bayesian posterior prediction with respect to the synthetic prior, without an explicit likelihood or posterior computation at test time. This interpretation requires two qualifications: the posterior is conditional on the implicit prior used in pretraining, and the quality of the approximation depends on network capacity and training.

\textbf{Inference as in-context learning.} At inference time, the observed data $\mathcal{D}_n$ is supplied as context and the candidate point $x$ as the query. A single forward pass returns $q_\phi(y \mid x, \mathcal{D}_n)$, with no gradient updates and no per-task hyperparameter fitting. This predictive distribution has the same form as $p(y \mid x, \mathcal{D}_n)$ in \Cref{sec:bayesian-optimization}, so a TFM can take the place of the GP in \Cref{alg:bo} without any other change.

\textbf{Categorical inputs.} TabPFN-3.5 accepts numerical and categorical features directly. No kernel is specified, and no similarity between levels has to be encoded by the user. Categorical columns must be declared as such, since otherwise they may be treated as numerical. According to the technical report, the per-cell encodings of TabPFN-3.5 were revised to improve the handling of high-cardinality categorical features, and the synthetic prior was adjusted toward such data. %Two points are relevant for BO. First, no kernel fixes in advance how levels are related or how a categorical variable changes the function over the continuous variables. Whether this structure can be recovered from tens of observations is an empirical question, which is examined in Chapter 4. Second, the benchmarks reported by the developers span tiny to million-row tables, but the smallest dataset in TabArena has about 500 training rows, which is above the budget of a typical BO run. Those results therefore do not directly show performance at BO sample sizes.

\textbf{Regression output and model details.} For regression, TabPFN-3.5 predicts a distribution over fixed bins covering the range of the target. The predictive distribution is therefore discrete and piecewise constant, rather than Gaussian. Its mean, variance, quantiles, and tail probabilities can be computed directly from the bin probabilities. TabPFN-3.5 uses a single checkpoint of about 220 million parameters trained jointly for classification and regression. By default it averages predictions over eight estimators applied to different permutations of the data. Its recommended limits are one million training rows and 6,000 features, which are recommendations for strong performance and not computational limits. Both far exceed the budgets and dimensions of the problems considered here.

\textbf{Use as a BO surrogate.} Because a TFM provides a full predictive distribution, it can serve as a probabilistic surrogate within BO. TabPFN-based surrogates have been explored in BO for high-dimensional problems \citep{ICLR2026_b54bafb9} and in a zero-shot setting \citep{Rogers2026}. Two details matter for statistical interpretation. First, the predictive distribution is over the noisy observation $y$ rather than the latent $f(x)$, which parallels the distinction between the predictive variance $\sigma_n^2(x) + \sigma^2$ and the posterior variance $\sigma_n^2(x)$ of the GP in \Cref{subsec:gaussian-process}. Second, the model returns a marginal distribution for each query point and does not provide a joint posterior over several points.

\textbf{Limitations.}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{Implicit prior.} The prior is fixed during pretraining and cannot be inspected or adjusted the way a kernel can. If the objective differs strongly from the synthetic prior, the predictive distribution may be misleading \citep{pmlr-v202-nagler23a}.
  \item \textit{Calibration under sequential sampling.} Reported evidence of good predictive distributions comes from benchmarks with i.i.d. data, such as ScoringBench. In BO the data are collected adaptively and concentrate near promising regions, so calibration cannot be assumed and has to be examined empirically.
  \item \textit{Marginal predictions.} Without joint samples, acquisition strategies that rely on them, such as Thompson sampling, are not directly available.
  \item \textit{Computational resources.} Each prediction requires a forward pass of a large transformer, which in practice calls for a GPU. %The open-weight checkpoints are released under a non-commercial license.
\end{itemize}

\section{Acquisition Functions}\label{sec:acquisition-functions}

The acquisition function $\alpha(\boldsymbol{x}; \mathcal{D}_n)$ in \eqref{eq:next-query} converts the output of the surrogate into a score that ranks candidate points by their usefulness for the next evaluation. Using it in BO involves two separate tasks. The first is to define the score, which depends only on the surrogate and is the subject of \Cref{subsec:expected-improvement}. The second is to find its maximizer over $\mathcal{X}$, which in a mixed space is a nontrivial problem of its own and is discussed in \Cref{subsec:acquisition-optimization-mixed-spaces}. The acquisition function and its optimizer are modular components.

\subsection{Expected Improvement}\label{subsec:expected-improvement}

Since the objective is maximized, let $y_n^* = \max_{i \le n} y_i$ denote the best observation so far. Probability of improvement (PI) scores a point by the probability that it improves on $y_n^*$ but ignores the size of the improvement \citep{10.1115/1.3653121}, and the upper confidence bound (UCB) trades off the predicted mean against the uncertainty through a parameter that must be chosen \citep{3104322.3104451}. This thesis uses expected improvement (EI), which accounts for both the probability and the size of the improvement and requires no additional parameter \citep{646296.687872, jones1998efficient}:

\begin{equation}
  \alpha_{\mathrm{EI}}(\boldsymbol{x}) = \mathbb{E}_{f(\boldsymbol{x}) \mid \mathcal{D}_n}\big[\max(y_n^* - f(\boldsymbol{x}),\, 0)\big],
  \label{eq:ei-definition}
\end{equation}

where the expectation is taken with respect to the posterior of $f(\boldsymbol{x})$ given $\mathcal{D}_n$. For a GP, this posterior is $\mathcal{N}(\mu_n(\boldsymbol{x}), \sigma_n^2(\boldsymbol{x}))$ as given in \eqref{eq:gp-posterior-mean} and \eqref{eq:gp-posterior-variance}, and the expectation has a closed form. With $z = (y_n^* - \mu_n(\boldsymbol{x})) / \sigma_n(\boldsymbol{x})$,

\begin{equation}
  \alpha_{\mathrm{EI}}(\boldsymbol{x}) = \sigma_n(\boldsymbol{x})\big[z\,\Phi(z) + \phi(z)\big],
  \label{eq:ei-closed-form}
\end{equation}

where $\Phi$ and $\phi$ denote the standard normal distribution and density functions. The two terms reflect the exploitation–exploration trade-off. The first grows as the predicted mean falls below $y_n^*$, and the second grows with the predictive uncertainty.

TabPFN does not return the posterior of $f(\boldsymbol{x})$ but a predictive distribution for the observation $y$ over fixed bins. This distribution is used in place of the posterior, and the expectation in \eqref{eq:ei-definition} is evaluated by summing over bins. Let bin $k$ span $[b_k, b_{k+1}]$ with width $w_k$ and probability mass $p_k(\boldsymbol{x})$, and assume that the mass is spread uniformly within each bin. Then

\begin{equation}
  \alpha_{\mathrm{EI}}(\boldsymbol{x}) = \sum_k p_k(\boldsymbol{x})\, e_k, \qquad
  e_k =
  \begin{cases}
  y_n^* - \tfrac{b_k + b_{k+1}}{2}, & y_n^* \ge b_{k+1},\\[2pt]
  \dfrac{(y_n^* - b_k)^2}{2 w_k}, & b_k < y_n^* < b_{k+1},\\[4pt]
  0, & y_n^* \le b_k.
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}

The substitution is exact when evaluations are noiseless. When they are noisy, the predictive distribution of $y$ is wider than the posterior of $f(\boldsymbol{x})$ by the observation noise, so the exploration value of uncertain points is overestimated. %The effect of this approximation is examined in the experiments.

\subsection{Acquisition Optimization in Mixed Spaces}\label{subsec:acquisition-optimization-mixed-spaces}

The next query point is the solution of the global problem in \eqref{eq:next-query}. In a continuous space, this maximization is relatively straightforward and is usually solved with first or second order methods started from several random initial points. In a mixed space, the gradient of $\alpha$ is not defined with respect to the categorical variables, so gradient-based optimizers cannot be applied directly. The problem is further complicated by the structure of $\mathcal{X}$, which combines a continuous part with a combinatorial part of $\prod_{j=1}^{m} K_j$ configurations. In addition, the acquisition function is typically multimodal and may contain flat regions, so the quality of the search affects the quality of the selected point.

\textbf{Zero-order search.} A common approach is to use zero-order methods, which rely only on evaluations of $\alpha$. Since $\alpha$ is cheaper to evaluate than $f$, these methods can afford many evaluations per iteration. %Two of them are described here.

\textit{Genetic algorithm.} A population of candidate points is evolved over several generations. In each generation, parents are selected according to their acquisition values, and offspring are produced by crossover, which recombines the variables of two parents, and by mutation, which changes the level of a categorical variable or perturbs a continuous variable. The fittest individuals survive to the next generation, and the best point found during the search is returned \citep{NEURIPS2020_b19aa25f}.

\textit{Simulated annealing.} A single current point is maintained, and at each step a neighbor is proposed by changing the level of one categorical variable and perturbing the continuous variables. A neighbor that improves $\alpha$ is accepted, and a worse neighbor is accepted with probability $\exp(-\Delta / T)$, where $\Delta$ is the loss in acquisition value and $T$ is a temperature that decreases during the run. The search can therefore leave local maxima early on and becomes increasingly greedy later \citep{doi:10.1126/science.220.4598.671, pmlr-v80-baptista18a}.

Other options combine a discrete search over the categorical part with gradient descent on the continuous part. Casmopolitan \citep{pmlr-v139-wan21b} alternates hill climbing and gradient descent, and CoCaBO \citep{pmlr-v119-ru20a} selects the categorical configuration with a multi-armed bandit. Both require the acquisition function to be differentiable with respect to the continuous variables. Zero-order methods, in contrast, can be paired with any surrogate.

\textbf{Trust regions.} Applied to the whole search space, these optimizers face a difficulty that grows with dimensionality. The surrogate must be accurate over all of $\mathcal{X}$, yet it is fitted from few observations, and its predictions far from the data are unreliable. A trust region addresses this by restricting the acquisition search to a neighborhood of a center point, usually the best point found so far. The idea was developed for continuous BO \citep{eriksson2019scalable} and extended to mixed spaces by Casmopolitan \citep{pmlr-v139-wan21b}. With $\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathrm{TR}} = (\boldsymbol{x}_c^{\mathrm{TR}}, \boldsymbol{x}_d^{\mathrm{TR}})$ the center of the region, a trust region for a mixed space is

\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{TR}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathrm{TR}}) = \big\{ \boldsymbol{x} \in \mathcal{X} : d_H(\boldsymbol{x}_d, \boldsymbol{x}_d^{\mathrm{TR}}) \le L_d, \;\; \lVert \boldsymbol{x}_c - \boldsymbol{x}_c^{\mathrm{TR}} \rVert_\infty \le L_c \big\},
  \label{eq:trust-region}
\end{equation}

where $d_H$ counts the categorical variables whose levels differ from those of the center, $L_d$ bounds the number of categorical variables that may change, and $L_c$ is the half-width of the box around the continuous center. The acquisition function is maximized over $\mathrm{TR}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathrm{TR}})$ instead of $\mathcal{X}$. %The size of the region is adapted during the run, expanding while the search keeps improving the best observation and shrinking when it does not.

The size of the region is adapted during the run. It expands after consecutive iterations that improve the best observation, and it shrinks after consecutive iterations that fail to do so. When it becomes too small, the search is restarted. The center is moved to the best point found so far. In this way, the search focuses on a promising region while remaining able to expand when progress is made.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related-work}

%Existing work on mixed-variable BO can be organized by how it addresses the difficulties in Section 2.2. Most methods are combinations of a surrogate and an acquisition optimizer, so the discussion follows the same split between the two components used in Sections 2.3 and 2.4.

\textbf{One-hot encoding.} A common approach is to convert each categorical variable into a one-hot vector and to apply a standard GP, as in the widely used packages Spearmint \citep{NIPS2012_05311655} and GPyOpt \citep{pmlr-v51-gonzalez16a}. This ignores that the objective is defined only on a small subset of the encoded space. It also increases the input dimension and makes the acquisition function harder to optimize, since the encoded points are far apart and the landscape contains many flat regions \citep{pmlr-v70-rana17a}.

\textbf{GP with kernels for categorical inputs.} A second group keeps the GP and replaces the kernel. CoCaBO \citep{pmlr-v119-ru20a} combines an overlap kernel on the categorical part with a kernel on the continuous part and selects the categorical configuration with a multi-armed bandit. Casmopolitan \citep{pmlr-v139-wan21b} uses a transformed overlap kernel together with trust regions and optimizes the acquisition function with hill climbing. BODi \citep{pmlr-v206-deshwal23a} embeds categorical inputs into a continuous space through a Hamming-distance-preserving dictionary representation. COMBO \citep{NEURIPS2019_2cb6b103} uses a diffusion kernel over a graph of categorical configurations and targets purely categorical problems. EXP3BO \citep{NEURIPS2018_cc709032} handles the setting in which the continuous variables are specific to a category by fitting a separate GP for each category, which shares no information across categories and therefore suits only a small number of categories.

\textbf{Tree-based methods.} Random forests handle categorical and continuous variables natively and are the surrogate of SMAC \citep{10.1007/978-3-642-25566-3_40}. Their uncertainty estimates are derived from the variability of the ensemble and are generally considered less reliable than a GP posterior. The tree-structured Parzen estimator (TPE) \citep{NIPS2011_86e8f7ab} models the density of good and bad configurations instead of the objective and does not return a predictive distribution.

\textbf{Frameworks and benchmarks.} MCBO \citep{NEURIPS2023_dbc4b67c} provides a modular library in which surrogates, acquisition functions, acquisition optimizers, and trust regions can be combined freely, together with a benchmark suite of synthetic and real tasks. Under this view, the methods above are particular combinations of components, as summarized in Table 2.2. BoTorch \citep{NEURIPS2020_f5b1b89d} is a general library that includes the kernel of Wan et al. (2021) but is aimed mainly at continuous and discrete spaces. The surrogates supported by MCBO are predominantly Gaussian processes with different kernels, so surrogates of other families are less represented in this setting.

\textbf{BO with prior-data fitted networks.} The most closely related work uses PFNs and tabular foundation models as surrogates. PFNs4BO \citep{pmlr-v202-muller23a} trains a prior-data fitted network for BO. GIT-BO \citep{ICLR2026_b54bafb9} uses TabPFN for high-dimensional BO and computes an upper confidence bound from the predictive mean and standard deviation of TabPFN, and \citet{Rogers2026} study a zero-shot BO method based on TabPFN. As far as the cited descriptions indicate, these works do not examine mixed spaces with several categorical variables within a controlled comparison of surrogates. %[Claim to be verified against the full texts.]
