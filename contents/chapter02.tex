\section{Bayesian Optimization}

Consider the problem of minimizing a black-box function $f: \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}$ over a mixed search space that combines continuous and categorical variables. A point is denoted as $x = (x_c, x_d)$, where $x_c$ contains $d_c$ continuous variables and $x_d$ contains $m$ categorical variables. The search space is

\begin{equation*}
  \mathcal{X} = \mathcal{X}_c \times \mathcal{X}_d, \qquad \mathcal{X}_c \subset \mathbb{R}^{d_c}, \qquad \mathcal{X}_d = \prod_{j=1}^{m} \{1, \dots, K_j\},
\end{equation*}

where $\mathcal{X}_c$ is compact and the $j$-th categorical variable takes one of $K_j$ levels. The levels are nominal, with no natural ordering or distance between them. The goal is to find

\begin{equation}
  x^* = \arg\min_{x \in \mathcal{X}} f(x).
\end{equation}

Purely continuous and purely categorical problems are special cases of this formulation. Integer and ordinal variables can be treated as either continuous or categorical variables.

The function $f$ is a black box: it has no known analytic form and its gradient is unavailable. It can be accessed only by querying a chosen point $x$, which returns an observation corrupted by noise,

\begin{equation}
  y = f(x) + \epsilon, \qquad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2),
\end{equation}

where the noiseless case corresponds to $\sigma^2 = 0$. Each evaluation is often expensive. Examples include training a machine learning model and characterizing material properties, so that the total budget is often limited to tens or hundreds of evaluations. Such problems are referred to as expensive black-box optimization. In a mixed space, this budget may be smaller than the number of categorical combinations $\prod_{j=1}^{m} K_j$.

Gradient-based methods, such as gradient descent and Newton's method, require derivative information and are therefore inapplicable. In a mixed space they are further restricted because the gradient is not even defined for categorical variables. Derivative-free methods rely only on function values and can be used instead. These include grid search and random search \citep{JMLR:v13:bergstra12a}, direct search methods such as Nelder-Mead \citep{10.1093/comjnl/7.4.308}, and population-based methods such as genetic algorithms and CMA-ES \citep{6790628}. However, none of these methods builds a global model of $f$. Grid search and random search ignore past evaluations when choosing the next point, and the other methods use only the current candidates or a summary of them to decide the next step. As a result, hundreds or thousands of evaluations are often required to converge, which is incompatible with the budget of expensive black-box problems. A method for this setting should instead make use of all past observations to decide where to evaluate next.

Bayesian optimization (BO) is a sequential, model-based strategy designed for this setting \citep{Močkus1975, jones1998efficient, 7352306, frazier2018tutorial}. Instead of probing $f$ blindly, BO maintains a probabilistic belief about $f$ and updates it after every evaluation. Let $\mathcal{D}_n = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ denote the observations collected after $n$ evaluations. At each iteration, BO uses $\mathcal{D}_n$ to decide where to query next in two steps:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Surrogate model.} A probabilistic model is fitted to $\mathcal{D}_n$ and yields a predictive distribution $p(y \mid x, \mathcal{D}_n)$ at any candidate point $x \in \mathcal{X}$. This distribution summarizes both the predicted value of $f(x)$ through its mean and the associated uncertainty through its spread. Because the surrogate is cheap to evaluate, it serves as an approximation to the expensive $f$.
  \item \textbf{Acquisition function.} A function $\alpha(x; \mathcal{D}_n)$ computed from the predictive distribution scores each candidate point by how useful it would be to evaluate next. Points with a promising predicted value receive high scores, which reflects exploitation, and so do points with high uncertainty, which reflects exploration. The next query point is
  \begin{equation}
    x_{n+1} = \arg\max_{x \in \mathcal{X}} \alpha(x; \mathcal{D}_n).
  \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

Since $\alpha$ is inexpensive, the inner maximization costs far less than a single evaluation of $f$. In a mixed space this maximization is itself a mixed continuous and combinatorial problem, which is treated in Section 2.3.2. The overall procedure is summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:bo}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
  \caption{Bayesian Optimization}
  \label{alg:bo}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require evaluation budget $N$, initial design size $n_0$, surrogate model, acquisition function $\alpha$
    \State Sample initial points $\boldsymbol{x}_1, \dots, \boldsymbol{x}_{n_0} \in \mathcal{X}$, evaluate $y_i = f(\boldsymbol{x}_i) + \epsilon_i$, and set $\mathcal{D}_{n_0} = \{(\boldsymbol{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n_0}$
    \For{$n = n_0, \dots, N-1$}
      \State Fit the surrogate to $\mathcal{D}_n$ to obtain $p(y \mid \boldsymbol{x}, \mathcal{D}_n)$
      \State Select $\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1} = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathcal{X}} \alpha(\boldsymbol{x}; \mathcal{D}_n)$
      \State Evaluate $y_{n+1} = f(\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}) + \epsilon_{n+1}$
      \State Update $\mathcal{D}_{n+1} = \mathcal{D}_n \cup \{(\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}, y_{n+1})\}$
    \EndFor
    \Ensure the best observed point, $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^* = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{(\boldsymbol{x}_i, y_i) \in \mathcal{D}_N} y_i$
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The separation of the surrogate from the acquisition function is a key design feature. The surrogate encodes the belief about $f$, while the acquisition function encodes how to act on that belief. The two components are modular: any surrogate that returns a predictive distribution can be paired with any acquisition function. This thesis exploits this modularity by replacing the surrogate while keeping the rest of the BO loop unchanged.

\section{Surrogate Models}

The surrogate model turns the observations $\mathcal{D}_n$ into a belief about $f$. For use within BO, it has to return a predictive distribution $p(y \mid \boldsymbol{x}, \mathcal{D}_n)$ rather than a point estimate, since the uncertainty drives exploration. In a mixed space it must in addition handle categorical inputs. This section reviews two surrogates: the Gaussian process, which is the standard choice, and tabular foundation models, which have recently emerged as an alternative.

\subsection{Gaussian Process}

A Gaussian process (GP) is a distribution over functions such that any finite collection of function values is jointly Gaussian \citep{rasmussen2006gaussian}. It is written as

\begin{equation}
  f(\boldsymbol{x}) \sim \mathcal{GP}\bigl(\mu_0(\boldsymbol{x}),\, k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}')\bigr),
\end{equation}

where $\mu_0(\boldsymbol{x}) = \mathbb{E}[f(\boldsymbol{x})]$ is the prior mean function and $k(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{x}') = \mathrm{Cov}(f(\boldsymbol{x}), f(\boldsymbol{x}'))$ is the kernel. The mean is commonly set to a constant after standardizing the data, so the kernel carries most of the modelling assumptions. In a mixed space, the kernel has to define similarity for both parts of $\boldsymbol{x} = (\boldsymbol{x}_c, \boldsymbol{x}_d)$.

\textbf{Kernels for the continuous part.} Common choices are the squared exponential kernel and the Matérn family of kernels \citep{rasmussen2006gaussian}, usually with one length-scale per dimension. These kernels encode smoothness and a decay of correlation with distance, and are written $k_c(\boldsymbol{x}_c, \boldsymbol{x}_c')$ below.

\textbf{Kernels for the categorical part.} Distance has no meaning between nominal levels, so kernels for categorical inputs measure only whether levels match. The overlap kernel \citep{pmlr-v119-ru20a} is

\begin{equation}
  k_d(\boldsymbol{x}_d, \boldsymbol{x}_d') = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{j=1}^{m} \lambda_j \, \delta\big(x_{d,j},\, x_{d,j}'\big),
\end{equation}

where $\delta$ is the Kronecker delta and $\lambda_j \ge 0$ is a weight that plays the role of an automatic relevance determination parameter. It measures the extent to which two inputs share the same levels, and a variable with a small $\lambda_j$ contributes little to the similarity. The transformed overlap kernel \citep{pmlr-v139-wan21b} applies the exponential function to the overlap kernel, which increases its expressive power. \citet{pmlr-v206-deshwal23a} proposed the Hamming embedding via dictionary kernel (HED), which transforms categorical configurations into continuous representations to enable the use of standard kernel functions. % The diffusion kernel (Oh et al., 2019) defines similarity through a graph over categorical configurations and is designed for purely categorical problems.

\textbf{Combining the two parts.} The continuous and categorical kernels can be combined by a product, a sum, or a mixture of the two. With the product form,

\begin{equation}
  k(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{x}') = \sigma_f^2\, k_c(\boldsymbol{x}_c, \boldsymbol{x}_c')\, k_d(\boldsymbol{x}_d, \boldsymbol{x}_d'),
\end{equation}

where $\sigma_f^2$ is the signal variance, two inputs are highly correlated only when they are close in the continuous part and agree on many of the categorical variables. The sum form instead allows information to be shared across levels through the continuous part alone. A mixture of the two lets the data decide how strongly the categorical and continuous effects interact \citep{pmlr-v119-ru20a}.

\textbf{Posterior and hyperparameter estimation.} Let $\boldsymbol{X} = (\boldsymbol{x}_1, \dots, \boldsymbol{x}_n)^\top$, $\boldsymbol{y} = (y_1, \dots, y_n)^\top$, $K = [k(\boldsymbol{x}_i, \boldsymbol{x}_j)]_{i,j}$, and $\boldsymbol{k}(\boldsymbol{x}) = (k(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{x}_1), \dots, k(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{x}_n))^\top$. The posterior of $f(\boldsymbol{x})$ given $\mathcal{D}_n$ is Gaussian with

\begin{align}
  \mu_n(\boldsymbol{x}) &= \mu_0(\boldsymbol{x}) + \boldsymbol{k}(\boldsymbol{x})^\top (K + \sigma^2 I)^{-1} (\boldsymbol{y} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0), \\
  \sigma_n^2(\boldsymbol{x}) &= k(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{x}) - \boldsymbol{k}(\boldsymbol{x})^\top (K + \sigma^2 I)^{-1} \boldsymbol{k}(\boldsymbol{x}),
\end{align}

where $\boldsymbol{\mu}_0 = (\mu_0(\boldsymbol{x}_1), \dots, \mu_0(\boldsymbol{x}_n))^\top$. The predictive distribution of a new noisy observation is $\mathcal{N}(\mu_n(\boldsymbol{x}), \sigma_n^2(\boldsymbol{x}) + \sigma^2)$. The hyperparameters $\boldsymbol{\theta} = (\sigma_f^2, \ell_1, \dots, \ell_{d_c}, \lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m, \sigma^2)$ are typically estimated by maximizing the log marginal likelihood,

\begin{equation}
  \log p(\boldsymbol{y} \mid \boldsymbol{X}, \boldsymbol{\theta}) = -\tfrac{1}{2} (\boldsymbol{y} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)^\top (K + \sigma^2 I)^{-1} (\boldsymbol{y} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0) - \tfrac{1}{2} \log \lvert K + \sigma^2 I \rvert - \tfrac{n}{2} \log 2\pi.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Limitations.} Despite its popularity, the GP faces several difficulties in a mixed space.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{Fixed notion of similarity.} The overlap kernel treats every pair of differing levels as equally dissimilar. Structure among levels, such as two levels that behave alike, can be learned only through the data, which are scarce.
  \item \textit{Many hyperparameters from few observations.} Each continuous dimension and each categorical variable has its own parameter. The marginal likelihood (2.9) is non-convex in $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ and can be poorly identified from few observations, so the fitted surrogate can be sensitive to the estimation procedure.
  \item \textit{Assumed interaction structure.} The choice among product, sum, and mixture kernels encodes an assumption about how the categorical variables change the function over the continuous variables. A wrong assumption can mislead the search.
  \item \textit{Cost and dimensionality.} Evaluating the posterior (2.7)–(2.8) and the marginal likelihood (2.9) costs $O(n^3)$ time. This is rarely an issue for tens of observations but grows with the budget, and kernel-based similarity degrades as the dimension increases.
\end{itemize}

These difficulties motivate surrogates that require less manual specification of similarity between inputs.
